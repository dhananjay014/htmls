Evaluation · ranking, discrimination, and calibration

Four metrics. Four different questions.

DCG and NDCG inspect the order of a recommendation list. AUC checks whether positives outrank negatives across thresholds. Normalized Entropy asks whether predicted probabilities deserve to be believed.

The first rule of metric reading

Before comparing two numbers, name the unit being evaluated: one query, one user, all impressions, one market, one day, or a weighted mixture. The same formula can answer a different question after aggregation.

Step 1 · Identify the evaluation object

A metric is a question plus an aggregation rule.

The formulas become much easier once you identify what is ordered, what is labeled, and which examples are allowed to interact.

MetricInputComparison neighborhoodBestMain invariance
DCG@kOne ranked list + graded labelsRanks 1 through kHigher; scale depends on labelsNone across queries with different gain mass
NDCG@kOne ranked list + graded labelsActual list versus ideal list1Normalizes available gain within a query
ROC AUCBinary labels + scoresEvery positive-negative pair1Strictly increasing score transforms
NEBinary labels + probabilitiesModel log loss versus constant CTR0Normalized for background-rate entropy

Metric directions are not uniform

Higher is better

DCG, NDCG, and AUC reward useful order. NDCG and AUC are usually bounded by 1; raw DCG is not query-comparable without care.

Lower is better

NE is a normalized loss. \(0\) is perfect prediction, \(1\) matches the constant-background-rate baseline, and values above \(1\) are worse than that baseline.

Question check

A monotonic transformation preserves every item ordering but makes probabilities more extreme. Which metrics must stay unchanged?

AUC stays unchanged because every positive-negative ordering is unchanged. DCG/NDCG also stay unchanged if the resulting item ranking stays unchanged. NE can change substantially because it evaluates the probability values themselves.
Step 2 · Accumulate discounted gain

DCG pays more for relevance near the top.

A relevance label becomes gain. Rank becomes a discount. DCG is the sum of gain multiplied by position weight.

DCG asks

“Given this exact ranked list, how much relevance did the user receive before patience ran out?”

Choose the convention before calculating

\[ \operatorname{DCG}@k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{g(r_i)}{\log_2(i+1)}, \qquad g(r)=2^r-1\ \text{in this guide}. \]

Linear gain

\(g(r)=r\). A grade-3 item is worth three grade-1 items before discounting. Scikit-learn’s dcg_score directly discounts the supplied relevance values.

Exponential gain

\(g(r)=2^r-1\). Grades \(0,1,2,3\) become gains \(0,1,3,7\), making highly relevant items disproportionately valuable.

Step 3 · Normalize by the ideal list

NDCG measures recovered opportunity.

IDCG sorts the same relevance labels into their best possible order. Dividing by IDCG asks what fraction of available discounted gain the model recovered.

Ideal ordering\[\operatorname{IDCG}@k=\operatorname{DCG}@k\text{ after sorting labels from high to low}\]
Normalize\[\operatorname{NDCG}@k=\frac{\operatorname{DCG}@k}{\operatorname{IDCG}@k}\]

Same labels, best ordering

The actual list has labels \([3,0,2,2,1]\). The ideal list is \([3,2,2,1,0]\), with gains \([7,3,3,1,0]\).

\[ \operatorname{IDCG}@5 =7+\frac{3}{\log_2 3}+\frac{3}{\log_2 4}+\frac{1}{\log_2 5} =10.8235. \] \[ \operatorname{NDCG}@5=\frac{10.1789}{10.8235}=\boxed{0.9404}. \]

At \(k=3\), the irrelevant rank-2 item is expensive: NDCG is \(0.8179\). At \(k=5\), later relevant items recover much of the available gain, raising NDCG to \(0.9404\). Always report the cutoff.

No relevant item

If \(\operatorname{IDCG}@k=0\), the ratio is undefined mathematically. Pipelines need an explicit convention: skip the query, return 0, or report it separately.

Tied model scores

A tie does not define one unique order. Libraries may average over possible tie orderings or accept an optimization that assumes ties do not occur.

Cutoff check

Can NDCG@10 and NDCG@100 disagree about which model is better?

Yes. NDCG@10 only rewards the first ten positions; NDCG@100 also sees deeper ranking quality. The model with the better head need not have the better tail.
Step 4 · Evaluate binary discrimination

AUC counts correctly ordered positive-negative pairs.

The ROC curve sweeps a threshold. The equivalent pairwise view is usually easier to calculate by hand: choose one positive and one negative, then ask which received the higher score.

\[ \operatorname{AUC} =\Pr(s^+>s^-)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(s^+=s^-). \]

With \(n_+\) positives and \(n_-\) negatives, there are \(n_+n_-\) cross-class pairs. A correct order earns 1, a tie usually earns \(0.5\), and a reversal earns 0.

Threshold-free is not decision-free

AUC summarizes every threshold, but a deployed system still needs an operating point with business-specific false-positive and false-negative costs.

AUC is not top-k

A reversal near the bottom and a reversal near the top each affect a positive-negative pair. AUC does not directly encode user attention at rank 1.

Step 5 · Evaluate probabilities against the base rate

Normalized Entropy makes log loss relative.

Here NE means Normalized Entropy—more precisely normalized cross-entropy or normalized log loss—as defined in Meta’s click-prediction work. Lower is better.

Model log loss\[ L=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right] \]
Background entropy\[ H(p_0)=-\left[p_0\ln p_0+(1-p_0)\ln(1-p_0)\right] \]
\[ \operatorname{NE}=\frac{L}{H(p_0)}. \]

In the Facebook paper, \(p_0\) is the average empirical CTR of the training data. A model that predicts \(p_0\) for every impression has \(\operatorname{NE}=1\) when evaluated under the matching distribution.

Complete five-impression example

Let training background CTR be \(p_0=0.4\). On this transparent evaluation slice, labels are \([1,0,1,0,0]\) and predicted click probabilities are \([0.8,0.3,0.6,0.2,0.1]\).

Why normalize?

Raw log loss is easier when the positive rate is extremely close to 0 or 1. Dividing by base-rate entropy expresses improvement relative to the difficulty of that background rate.

Why clip probabilities?

\(\ln(0)\) diverges. Production implementations clip \(p_i\) to \([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\), and the chosen \(\epsilon\), weighting, and base-rate window belong in the metric contract.

Step 6 · Separate ordering from probability quality

AUC can stay fixed while NE changes dramatically.

AUC only sees relative order. NE sees every numerical probability. Scale logits without changing their order and the two metrics tell different stories.

Why AUC is frozen

Multiplying every logit by a positive constant is strictly increasing. Every positive-negative pair keeps the same winner.

Why NE moves

Cross-entropy rewards confident correct probabilities and punishes confident mistakes. The negative example with a high score becomes increasingly expensive as scale grows.

Do not call AUC a calibration metric. Scores can produce excellent AUC while being unusable as probabilities. Conversely, post-hoc calibration can improve NE without changing AUC if it preserves order.
Step 7 · Match metrics to product behavior

Choose metrics from the decision surface backward.

The best offline metric is the one whose weighting resembles the product decision—then is validated against online outcomes.

Top slate qualityUse NDCG@k

When users consume an ordered page and graded relevance matters. Pick \(k\) from visible inventory or realistic attention depth.

Binary separationUse AUC

When you need threshold-independent discrimination over positives and negatives. Consider per-user or grouped AUC if global pairs hide personalization.

Probabilities drive valueUse NE + calibration

When bids, expected value, or downstream decisions depend on probability magnitude, not merely order.

Rare positives and head precisionAdd PR-AUC / Precision@k

ROC AUC can look comfortable when negatives dominate. Add a metric that exposes positive precision in the operating region.

A production evaluation sequence

1 · Freeze the metric contractLabels, attribution window, cutoff, gain, ties, weights.

A metric name without these choices is incomplete.

2 · Slice before averagingUser cohorts, markets, item age, sequence length.

Aggregate gains can conceal regressions where the product is most fragile.

3 · Add uncertaintyBootstrap by the independent unit.

Repeated impressions from one user are not independent rows.

4 · Validate onlineOffline metrics are proxies.

Check latency, diversity, satisfaction, revenue, and long-term effects in controlled experiments.

Step 8 · Audit the metric before trusting it

Most metric bugs live outside the formula.

Inconsistent NDCG gain

Linear and exponential gain can reverse conclusions. State \(g(r)\), log base, and cutoff in every dashboard definition.

Unlabeled does not always mean irrelevant

Implicit feedback is exposure-biased. An unclicked unseen item is not the same as a shown-and-skipped item.

Global AUC leakage

If one user has a higher base score than another, global cross-user pairs can be easy even when each user’s candidate order is poor.

AUC under class imbalance

AUC is mathematically pair-based, but the ROC view can underemphasize poor positive precision. Inspect PR curves and the deployed region.

Moving NE denominator

If background CTR is recomputed differently across experiments, NE changes for reasons unrelated to the model. Version the baseline window.

Missing probability clipping

One confidently wrong \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) yields infinite log loss. Define numerical clipping identically across systems.

Selection and position bias

Offline labels come from an older policy that chose what users saw. Better observed NDCG does not automatically imply better counterfactual ranking.

Point estimate worship

A change from 0.8112 to 0.8114 is not a conclusion without uncertainty, slice stability, and practical effect size.

Metric diagnosis

Model B has the same AUC as Model A, lower NE, and identical top-k ordering. What most likely improved?

Its probability calibration or confidence quality improved while its ordering stayed fixed. A monotonic calibration map is one possible cause.

1. Why is rank 1 undiscounted?
2. What does IDCG hold fixed?
3. Why can NDCG@3 and NDCG@5 differ sharply?
4. How does AUC treat score ties?
5. Why can calibration improve NE without changing AUC?
6. What does NE = 1 mean?

A monotonic map changes probabilities but preserves order. 6. Model log loss equals the constant-background-rate baseline under the stated metric contract.

Scope and convention notes

Gain convention

The main DCG/NDCG calculation uses \(2^r-1\). Some libraries and papers use raw relevance \(r\); numbers are not interchangeable.

Log base

DCG uses base-2 logarithms here. NE uses natural logarithms, but using another base in both numerator and denominator leaves the ratio unchanged.

NE baseline

The worked example uses \(p_0=0.4\) as a fixed training CTR. Production comparisons must version the baseline population and weighting.

AUC ties

The pair calculation awards half credit to equal scores. Framework behavior for ties, multiclass averaging, and sample weights should be verified explicitly.
