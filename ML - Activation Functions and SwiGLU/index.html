Neural-network foundations · from scalar curves to gated FFNs

An activation decides what a layer can express—and what gradient can return.

Sigmoid and tanh compress values into bounded ranges. ReLU removes that positive-side bottleneck. GELU and SiLU replace the hard threshold with smooth input-dependent weighting. SwiGLU goes one step further: it learns the candidate feature and its gate with separate projections.

Why insert a nonlinear activation?

Without nonlinearity, depth collapses \[W_3\!\left(W_2(W_1x+b_1)+b_2\right)+b_3 =W_{\text{effective}}x+b_{\text{effective}}\]

Composing affine maps still gives one affine map. An activation \(\phi\) breaks that collapse, so a stack can bend, gate, and combine features differently in different regions of the input space.

Bounded squashing

Sigmoid maps to \((0,1)\); tanh maps to \((-1,1)\). Useful for probabilities and bounded states, risky across deep hidden stacks.

Hard selection

ReLU passes positive values unchanged and maps negative values to exactly zero.

Soft selection

GELU and SiLU multiply the input by a smooth gate, preserving graded information near zero.

Memory hook

Sigmoid and tanh squash. ReLU clips. GELU and SiLU weigh. SwiGLU learns a separate feature and gate.

Bounded functions · where gradients become flat

Saturation means the output barely changes when the input moves.

Sigmoid and tanh are not “bad functions.” Their bounded ranges are valuable. The problem is using many saturated hidden layers, where the chain rule repeatedly multiplies by small derivatives.

Sigmoid\[\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\]\[\sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\]

Range \((0,1)\); maximum derivative \(0.25\).

Tanh\[\tanh(x)=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}\]\[\tanh'(x)=1-\tanh^2(x)\]

Range \((-1,1)\); derivative is \(1\) at zero but approaches zero at both tails.

Large negative input

Sigmoid approaches \(0\), tanh approaches \(-1\), and both derivatives approach \(0\).

Near zero

The curves still move. Sigmoid’s derivative peaks at \(0.25\); tanh’s peaks at \(1\).

Large positive input

Sigmoid approaches \(1\), tanh approaches \(1\), and both derivatives again approach \(0\).

A ten-layer activation-only thought experiment

At \(x=3\), the sigmoid derivative is about \(0.04518\). Isolating only the activation factors:

Ten saturated sigmoid factors \[(0.04518)^{10}\approx3.54\times10^{-14}\]

For tanh at \(x=3\), \(\tanh'(3)\approx0.009866\), so:

Ten saturated tanh factors \[(0.009866)^{10}\approx8.74\times10^{-21}\]
This is an isolation, not a full-network prediction.

Real gradients also multiply weight matrices, normalization Jacobians, residual paths, and loss derivatives. The calculation shows why repeatedly saturated activations are dangerous; it does not claim every deep sigmoid network has exactly this gradient.

Where these functions still belong

Sigmoid output

A binary probability must live in \((0,1)\). Sigmoid is a natural output map even when hidden layers use another activation.

Sigmoid gate

LSTM, GRU, and GLU gates intentionally need a soft fraction between zero and one.

Tanh state update

Bounded signed updates are useful in recurrent state dynamics.

Shallow networks

Saturation is much less severe when the chain is short and preactivations stay near zero.

Rectification · one unsaturated half-space

ReLU’s positive side passes signal and gradient without squashing.

Rectified linear unit \[\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x),\qquad \operatorname{ReLU}'(x)= \begin{cases}0&x\lt0\\1&x\gt0\end{cases}\]
Negative inputReLU(−1) = 0

The feature is exactly off and its local derivative is zero.

Positive inputReLU(3) = 3

The activation factor in backpropagation is exactly \(1\).

At zeroderivative undefined

Libraries choose a subgradient convention, commonly \(0\).

Why it was such an effective default

No positive-side saturation

Large positive features remain large, and the activation derivative stays \(1\).

Cheap operation

A comparison with zero is simpler than an exponential or Gaussian CDF.

Exact sparsity

Negative preactivations become exact zeros, creating input-dependent inactive units.

Piecewise-linear geometry

Different active-unit patterns partition input space into many affine regions.

The failure mode: a dead unit

If a unit’s preactivation stays negative for every training example, then both output and local derivative stay zero. Its incoming weights receive no gradient through that path.

Leaky alternative \[\operatorname{LeakyReLU}(x)= \begin{cases}x&x\gt0\\\alpha x&x\leq0\end{cases}, \qquad 0\lt\alpha\ll1\]
Balanced conclusion

ReLU is a strong, inexpensive baseline—not a universal winner. Smooth or gated activations can outperform it, while sparse ReLU variants can be attractive when inference efficiency matters.

Smooth weighting · GELU and SiLU

GELU and SiLU turn the hard switch into a graded gate.

GELU\[\operatorname{GELU}(x)=x\Phi(x)\]

\(\Phi(x)\) is the standard normal CDF. The input is weighted by a Gaussian probability.

SiLU / Swish-1\[\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x\sigma(x)\]

The sigmoid provides an input-dependent fraction, while multiplication keeps the output unbounded.

Common GELU approximation \[\operatorname{GELU}(x)\approx \frac{x}{2}\left(1+\tanh\!\left[\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left(x+0.044715x^3\right)\right]\right)\]

The derivatives explain the smooth transition

GELU derivative\[\operatorname{GELU}'(x)=\Phi(x)+x\phi(x)\]

\(\phi(x)\) is the standard normal density.

SiLU derivative\[\operatorname{SiLU}'(x)=\sigma(x)+x\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\]

The derivative can be slightly negative or slightly larger than \(1\).

Non-monotonic does not mean unstable.

GELU and SiLU have a shallow negative dip. That gives a narrow region where increasing \(x\) decreases the output. The effect is bounded and is part of their soft-selection behavior.

Backpropagation · what the local derivative can and cannot tell you

Activation derivatives are factors in the gradient—not the whole gradient.

One hidden stack \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_0} =\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_L} \prod_{\ell=L}^{1} \left(W_\ell^\top\operatorname{diag}\!\left[\phi'(z_\ell)\right]\right)\]

The activation contributes the diagonal derivative term. Weight singular values, normalization, residual addition, and the loss can shrink or amplify the product too.

Check the claim

Does ReLU guarantee that gradients never vanish?

No. Negative ReLU units have derivative zero, weight matrices can contract gradients, and other operations can shrink them. ReLU only removes positive-side saturation from the activation itself.
Transformer context · residual stream and normalization

The residual preserves the state; the FFN proposes an update.

Saying “Transformers do not need ReLU sparsity because they have normalization and residuals” is too compressed. Residuals and normalization do not replace sparsity. They make deep information and gradient flow less dependent on one activation’s hard zeroing behavior.

Forward path\[y=x+F(\operatorname{Norm}(x))\]

The block can learn a correction instead of rebuilding the whole representation.

Backward path\[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}=I+\frac{\partial F(\operatorname{Norm}(x))}{\partial x}\]

The identity term gives gradients a direct route around the nonlinear branch.

What normalization contributes

LayerNorm core, before learned scale and shift \[\operatorname{LN}(x)=\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}\]

Normalization controls the scale presented to a sublayer. That reduces uncontrolled drift and often keeps GELU or SiLU in regions with useful derivatives. RMSNorm controls root-mean-square scale without recentering. Neither operation creates ReLU-style exact sparsity by itself.

Residual

Preserves the old representation and adds a learned update.

Normalization

Controls the scale entering the update branch.

Activation

Shapes which candidate features the update branch expresses.

Better sentence

Transformers get much of their training stability and information flow from normalization and residual connections, so their FFNs can use smooth selection without relying on ReLU’s hard zeroing to make deep optimization workable.

Gated feed-forward networks · separate gate and content

SwiGLU learns what feature to create and how strongly to pass it.

A plain FFN projects once, applies one activation, and projects back. SwiGLU makes two independent input projections: one becomes a SiLU gate, the other becomes candidate content. Their elementwise product is then projected back to the residual width.

Plain activation FFN\[\operatorname{FFN}(x)=\phi(xW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2\]

One projected scalar determines both feature value and activation state.

SwiGLU FFN\[\operatorname{FFN}(x)=\left[\operatorname{SiLU}(xW_g)\odot(xW_v)\right]W_o\]

Gate and candidate content have separate learned projections.

Complete real-number example · one token through SwiGLU

Project twice, gate, multiply, project back, then add the residual.

Use one token state with \(d=3\) and a teaching hidden width \(m=4\). Biases are zero so every multiplication remains visible.

Input and dimensions \[x=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x:[1,3],\quad W_g,W_v:[3,4],\quad W_o:[4,3]\]
Three learned matrices \[ W_g=\begin{bmatrix} 1&0&-1&0.5\\ 0.5&1&0&-1\\ 0&0.5&1&0.5 \end{bmatrix},\qquad W_v=\begin{bmatrix} 1&-1&0.5&0\\ 0&1&1&-0.5\\ 0.5&0&1&1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ W_o=\begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0.5\\ 0&1&-0.5\\ 0.5&0.5&0\\ 0&0.25&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

1 · Gate projection \(a=xW_g\)

\(a_1\)\(1(1)+(-1)(0.5)+2(0)=0.5\)
\(a_2\)\(1(0)+(-1)(1)+2(0.5)=0\)
\(a_3\)\(1(-1)+(-1)(0)+2(1)=1\)
\(a_4\)\(1(0.5)+(-1)(-1)+2(0.5)=2.5\)
\[a=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0&1&2.5\end{bmatrix}\]

2 · Content projection \(b=xW_v\)

\(b_1\)\(1(1)+(-1)(0)+2(0.5)=2\)
\(b_2\)\(1(-1)+(-1)(1)+2(0)=-2\)
\(b_3\)\(1(0.5)+(-1)(1)+2(1)=1.5\)
\(b_4\)\(1(0)+(-1)(-0.5)+2(1)=2.5\)
\[b=\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&1.5&2.5\end{bmatrix}\]

3 · Apply the SiLU gate

\(\operatorname{SiLU}(0.5)\)\(0.5/(1+e^{-0.5})=0.3112\)
\(\operatorname{SiLU}(0)\)\(0/(1+e^0)=0\)
\(\operatorname{SiLU}(1)\)\(1/(1+e^{-1})=0.7311\)
\(\operatorname{SiLU}(2.5)\)\(2.5/(1+e^{-2.5})=2.3104\)
\[\operatorname{SiLU}(a)=\begin{bmatrix}0.3112&0&0.7311&2.3104\end{bmatrix}\]

4 · Gate × content

Elementwise multiplication \[ z=\operatorname{SiLU}(a)\odot b = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3112(2)&0(-2)&0.7311(1.5)&2.3104(2.5) \end{bmatrix} \] \[ z=\begin{bmatrix}0.6225&0&1.0966&5.7759\end{bmatrix} \]

5 · Output projection and residual

\(o_1\)\(0.6225(1)+0(0)+1.0966(0.5)+5.7759(0)=1.1708\)
\(o_2\)\(0.6225(0)+0(1)+1.0966(0.5)+5.7759(0.25)=1.9923\)
\(o_3\)\(0.6225(0.5)+0(-0.5)+1.0966(0)+5.7759(1)=6.0871\)
\[o=zW_o=\begin{bmatrix}1.1708&1.9923&6.0871\end{bmatrix}\] \[y=x+o=\begin{bmatrix}2.1708&0.9923&8.0871\end{bmatrix}\]
What the numbers show

The fourth gate value \(2.3104\) amplifies content \(2.5\) into \(5.7759\). The second gate is exactly zero here, so content \(-2\) contributes nothing. The output projection then mixes hidden channels back into the three residual dimensions.

Shape check

Why can the residual add \(x\) to \(o\), but not directly to \(z\)?

The input and projected output both have width \(d=3\). The gated hidden vector \(z\) has width \(m=4\). \(W_o:[4,3]\) restores the residual width before addition.

The important modern comparison is often plain FFN versus gated FFN.

Why the hidden width often becomes \(8d/3\)

Standard FFN, hidden width \(4d\)\[d(4d)+(4d)d=8d^2\]

Two matrices, biases omitted.

SwiGLU, hidden width \(m\)\[dm+dm+md=3dm\]

Two input projections and one output projection.

Match parameter budgets \[3dm=8d^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=\frac{8}{3}d\]

Do not reduce the story to “SiLU beats ReLU.”

Architecture, initialization, normalization, optimizer, scale, hardware kernels, sparsity goals, and parameter matching all matter. In Transformers, the multiplicative gated structure is often the larger change.

From saturation to SwiGLU in ten statements.

1 · NonlinearityAffine maps alone collapse.

An activation lets depth create input-dependent regions and gates.

2 · SaturationFlat output means a small derivative.

Long products of small derivatives can starve early layers.

3 · SigmoidUseful probability and gate, risky hidden stack.

Range \((0,1)\), derivative at most \(0.25\).

4 · TanhSigned and centered, still bounded.

Derivative is \(1\) at zero but vanishes in both tails.

5 · ReLUPass positive, delete negative.

Cheap and sparse; positive derivative \(1\); dead-unit risk.

6 · GELUWeight by Gaussian probability.

\(x\Phi(x)\) gives smooth soft selection.

7 · SiLUSelf-gate with a sigmoid.

\(x\sigma(x)\) keeps a broad, smooth negative region.

8 · ResidualPreserve state, learn an update.

The identity route carries information and gradient around the FFN.

9 · NormalizationControl the input scale.

It supports stable sublayers but does not create ReLU sparsity.

10 · SwiGLULearn content and gate separately.

Two input projections multiply, then one output projection restores width \(d\).

One-minute oral check

Why can SwiGLU be more expressive than replacing ReLU with SiLU in a standard FFN?

A standard FFN uses one projection before its activation, so the same hidden scalar is both candidate feature and gating signal. SwiGLU learns two projections and multiplies them, separating what content exists from how strongly it passes.

Accuracy and scope notes

Derivative products

The vanishing-gradient examples isolate activation derivatives. They deliberately hold all other Jacobian factors aside.

Architecture claims

“Preferred” is workload-dependent. The guide distinguishes standalone SiLU from the larger SwiGLU architectural change.

Teaching matrices

The numerical SwiGLU weights are original small values chosen for inspectable arithmetic, not trained parameters from a published model.

Parameter matching

The \(8d/3\) width follows from equating bias-free matrix parameter counts; production implementations round widths for hardware efficiency.
